Advice for parents • Talking about feelings

How to talk to your daughter about worry (without making it worse)

You can see the worry behind her eyes. You ask “what’s wrong?” and the door shuts anyway. As a kid’s life coach, I hear the same sentence from a Christian parent almost every week: “She won’t talk to me.” Here’s how to change that — starting tonight.

Before the words, a truth: an anxious girl is not refusing you. She’s protecting herself from a feeling she can’t explain. The conversation she won’t have at your kitchen table is often the one she can have sideways — in the car, walking the dog, hands busy washing-up. Connection first, eye contact never. That’s rule one.

Three sentences to retire (and what to say instead)

  • “There’s nothing to worry about.” True in your adult maths; untrue in her maths. It teaches her feelings are wrong, not that worries are small. Try instead: “That sounds like a big worry. Tell me where you feel it.”
  • “You’ll be fine!” Intended to comfort, experienced as “you don’t get it.” Try: “Whatever happens, we’ll handle it together.” That promise is designed to be true — which is what makes it calming.
  • “Don’t be daft, it’s just a sleepover.” Shame is jet-fuel for anxious thoughts. Try: “A lot of girls feel exactly this at 10. You’re not weird, you’re worried. Two different things.”

The four-question conversation that actually opens doors

Wait for a two-by-two moment (same side of the table, mood calm). Then walk these in order — slowly:

  1. “Where in your body do you feel it?” — Tummy? Chest? Head? Naming where the feeling sits helps calm it — it moves the feeling from panic to information.
  2. “What does the worry say will happen?” — Let her present your danger in her own words. Don’t correct yet. Curiosity first.
  3. “And what would you say to a friend having that thought?” — This is the trick I use in every session. Children almost always answer rationally for the friend — then pause when they realise it applies to them. That pause is the whole therapy of coaching, miniaturised at home.
  4. “What’s one tiny brave step for tomorrow?” — Choose it together. Small enough to actually happen: eye contact with the teacher, saying one sentence at break, sitting next to Zara.

Ask her feelings to God, not to fix

For a Christian parent, prayer is the anchor of the whole thing — with one adjustment in emphasis. Pray with her, not at her: “Father, Bea is worried about break time tomorrow. Please be as close to her as you promise, and remind her she is fearfully and wonderfully made.” Short, honest, in her own words when she’s ready. What this teaches her: worry is safe to bring to God and to you — which is precisely the courage a kid’s life coach is trying to build too.

“Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God.”PHILIPPIANS 4:6

When dinner-table talks stop being enough

If weeks of gentle openers go nowhere, or the worry keeps growing despite your best evenings, bring in a kid’s life coach. A neutral adult can draw out things a daughter can’t hand her mum — and in sessions at Shiny Minds, faith stays gently woven through the whole process. And one honest note, always: if what you’re seeing looks bigger than confidence-building, start with your GP. Coaching and clinical care are allies, not rivals.
