Advice for parents • Kid’s life coaching

What happens in a kid’s life coaching first session?

You have read the pages, looked at the calendar, and hovered over the button. The last question is usually the quiet one: what will actually happen to my daughter in that room? Fair question. Here is the honest walkthrough of a first session, from the kid’s life coach on the other side of the screen.

First, the reassurance most Christian parents need before anything else: a first session does not begin with a stranger asking your daughter to pour out her deepest fear. It begins with her telling me about her dog. Trust is built in that order, and a child can tell within sixty seconds whether an adult is genuinely interested or just working through a script.

Before we meet

You and I have a short conversation first, without her in the room. Not a report on her failings — a description of what you are seeing: the Sunday nights, the mornings, the things she has stopped doing. You tell me what you have already tried. I tell you honestly whether coaching is the right support or whether this needs your GP first. If it is not right, I will say so. Coaching should never be the thing you do because you could not get an appointment.

The first ten minutes: no agenda

When we meet, the first ten minutes belong to her. What she likes, what she is good at, who she lives with, what makes her laugh. I am listening for two things: the language she uses about herself, and the thing she is proud of. Both become tools later. A girl who can tell me she is good at drawing has already told me she is a person who makes things — and that is a doorway.

The middle: naming it, then moving it outside her

At some point I will ask, lightly, what it is like on a school morning. Then a very specific question that adults rarely ask: “Where do you feel it?” Tummy, chest, throat, head. Most girls have never been asked, and most answer immediately.

Then we give the worry a shape — a name, a colour, a character. “The Monday Cloud.” This is not babyish; it is clinically sensible. A feeling that lives inside her is her identity. A cloud that visits her is weather, and weather can be managed. From there we practise one tool together: a long-exhale breath, a five-senses countdown, a script for the cloakroom. One. Not five. A girl who leaves with five tools uses none.

The last ten minutes: one brave step

We choose a single small action for the week — something she would not otherwise do, but that she can imagine doing. Speaking to one person at break. Asking the teacher a question. Walking in without being carried by the worry. It must be hers, not yours. A step chosen by a parent is a task; a step chosen by her is courage.

What I tell you, and what I don’t

Afterwards you get the themes, not the transcript: what we worked on, the tool she chose, the brave step, and how you can support it at home. What she said in confidence stays in confidence, because a girl who learns her sessions are reported back word for word will stop being honest by week three. The exception is safety — if she discloses harm or risk, you will know immediately. That boundary is explained to her at the start, so nothing is a surprise.

“Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not turn from it.”PROVERBS 22:6

Faith in a session is never a lecture. It is a quiet assumption underneath the work: that she is made on purpose, that her worth is not up for review, and that she is not alone in the room. If she wants to pray, we pray. If she does not, she still gets the same hour of careful attention.

How many sessions?

Most girls I work with come for six to eight sessions, usually weekly at first. The aim is never dependence on me. It is a girl who has her own words for the worry, two or three tools she actually uses, and a parent who knows how to keep the work going on an ordinary Tuesday. If you would like to talk through whether that fits your daughter, the first thirty-minute conversation is free — and yes, it is with you, not her.
