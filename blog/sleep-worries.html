Night-time worries: helping an anxious girl sleep

By day she holds it together. At 9pm the questions start, and by 11pm you are both exhausted and nobody is sleeping. Night is where an anxious girl does her loudest thinking — and where a Christian parent feels most helpless. Here is what a kid’s life coach would change first.

Nothing has gone wrong at bedtime. That is the first thing to understand. Daytime gives a worried brain noise to hide behind — lessons, friends, screens, instructions. At night the noise stops, and the brain finally gets an appointment with everything it has been holding. The 9pm question is not a new worry. It is an old one that has been waiting all day for a quiet room.

The hour before bed matters more than the minute of

Most bedtime battles are actually wind-down failures. A girl who has been on a screen, in a bright room, or in the middle of a sibling argument until 8:55pm cannot switch off at 9:00pm. Build a sixty-minute runway:

  • Dim the lights an hour before bed. Light tells the brain it is still daytime.
  • Screens off — ideally in another room. This is the single change that moves the needle most.
  • Same three steps, same order, every night: bath or shower, teeth and pyjamas, one chapter or one prayer. Predictability is a nervous-system medicine.
  • Warm and heavy: a warm drink, a heavier blanket, socks if her feet get cold. Physical comfort is not a reward; it is a signal.

The worry appointment

If you try to stop her worrying at bedtime, you are asking her brain to do the hardest thing it knows. Instead, give the worry a scheduled slot — 6pm, at the kitchen table, for ten minutes. She tells you everything on her mind; you write it down. At bedtime, the answer becomes: “That is written down. We are not doing worries now; we are doing sleep. We will look at it tomorrow at six.”

This works because it is honest. You are not telling her the worry is silly. You are telling her it has a place, and that place is not her pillow.

What to say at 2am

The instinct is to fix, explain and reassure. At 2am, all three wake her up further. Keep it boring, brief and warm: “I’m here. It’s nighttime. Back to sleep.” Same words, same tone, every time. Walk her back to bed rather than staying in yours — not because you are hard-hearted, but because her bed has to become a safe place again and it cannot do that while she is in yours.

Tools that actually settle a body

  1. Breathe out longer than in — in for four, out for six, five times. The long exhale is the brake pedal of the nervous system.
  2. Squeeze and release — tense her toes, then legs, then hands, then shoulders, letting each go. Five rounds.
  3. Name the room — five things she can see, four she can touch, three she can hear. It pulls her out of her head and into the present.
  4. One small light, if she needs it. No shame in a nightlight at eleven. We are building sleep, not toughness.
“In peace I will lie down and sleep, for you alone, Lord, make me dwell in safety.”PSALM 4:8

Pray it slowly with her, in the dark, in your normal voice. Not a performance — a habit. Girls who learn that the night belongs to God stop treating 9pm as a court hearing.

When it is bigger than bedtime

See your GP if she is awake for hours most nights for more than six weeks, if she is exhausted and struggling at school, if she snores heavily or stops breathing, or if she is frightened of sleep itself. Anxiety and sleep problems feed each other, and a clinician should see the pattern early. Alongside that, a kid’s life coach can work on the daytime worry that keeps arriving at night — because the goal is not a child who sleeps because she is exhausted. It is a child who sleeps because her mind has somewhere safe to put its worries.
