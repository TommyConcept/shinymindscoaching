School refusal: a gentle plan for anxious girls

The uniform is on, the shoes are by the door, and then it starts: the tummy ache, the tears, the “please don’t make me”. If you are a Christian parent living this on repeat, this is for you. As a kid’s life coach, I want to start with the sentence that changes everything: she is not being naughty.

School refusal and truancy are two different things, and mixing them up is the fastest way to make it worse. Truancy usually means a child is avoiding school — often to be somewhere else. Refusal means she is avoiding a feeling: the spike of panic in the car park, the dread of the cloakroom, the shame of being seen crying. One is a behaviour problem. The other is an anxiety problem wearing a school uniform.

For an anxious girl, the morning is a physical event. Her heart rate rises before she is even awake properly. Her stomach genuinely hurts — anxiety lives in the gut, which is why so many of these girls are checked for tummy bugs before anyone checks for worry. She is not inventing it. She is reporting it.

What is happening inside her

  • The alarm is real. Her nervous system reads school as danger. That is not a decision she made; it is a reflex she has not learned to steer yet.
  • Avoidance rewards her. Stay home, and the panic drops instantly. Her brain files this as: that worked. Which is why each missed day makes the next one harder.
  • Shame piles on top. She knows other girls go in fine. She now has two problems: the fear, and the belief that she is the only one who has it.
  • Her identity starts to shrink. After a few weeks she stops saying “I’m worried about school” and starts saying “I’m the girl who can’t.” That sentence is the real work.

A morning plan that lowers the temperature

Nothing here is a trick. It is a staircase back, one small step at a time.

  1. Decide the night before, not at 8am. Morning is the worst possible time to negotiate — for both of you. Agree the next day’s plan at bedtime, calmly, and write it down where she can see it.
  2. One adult, one goodbye. Pass her to the same trusted person each day, with the same five words. Long goodbyes teach her that leaving is dangerous. Short ones teach her it is ordinary.
  3. Give her a script. Rehearse it at home: “My tummy feels wobbly. I’m going to tell Miss Jones and then sit with Poppy.” A girl with a plan has somewhere to put the feeling.
  4. Build a ladder, not a cliff. If full days are impossible, agree a smaller first rung: breakfast club only, then a morning, then a full day. Each rung should be uncomfortable but achievable.
  5. Praise the brave step, not the attendance. “You walked in even though your body said no — that is courage” is worth more than a full week achieved by force.

Four things that feel right and don’t work

Carrying her through the gate teaches her that she cannot walk in herself. Lecturing in the car park adds shame to a nervous system that is already at capacity. Removing all demand forever tells her brain the danger was real. And punishing the tummy ache is punishing a symptom — she will simply stop mentioning it.

“So do not fear, for I am with you; do not be dismayed, for I am your God. I will strengthen you and help you.”ISAIAH 41:10

Read that verse with her, not at her, and notice what it does not say. It does not say “there is nothing to fear”. It says I am with you. That is the difference between a Christian parent and a coach with a clipboard: we are not trying to talk her out of her fear, we are trying to walk into it beside her.

When to bring in more help

If she has missed more than a fortnight, if the mornings are involving physical illness, or if your whole household now organises itself around avoiding school, talk to your GP first — anxiety at this level deserves a clinical look. Then let a kid’s life coach work alongside that: a neutral adult who is not the person saying “shoes on”, who can name the fear, externalise it, and rehearse the brave step before Monday arrives.

School refusal is not a character flaw and it is not a parenting failure. It is a girl whose alarm system has learned the wrong lesson, and an adult who is willing to teach it a gentler one.
