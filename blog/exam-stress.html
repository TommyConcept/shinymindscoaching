Exam stress: helping your daughter revise without panic

She knows her tables. She can do the comprehension. So why is she in tears over a practice paper? Because exam stress is not a knowledge problem — it is a body problem. As a kid’s life coach, here is what I would change first for an anxious girl facing SATs, the 11+, or a big week of tests.

Watch the signs before the marks: stomach aches on test mornings, tears over homework that was fine last week, “I’m going to fail” said as fact rather than fear, sleep sliding, appetite changing, sudden perfectionism — rubbing out a whole page because one letter is wrong. None of that is laziness. All of it is a nervous system that has decided the stakes are life or death.

A nine-year-old cannot tell you that. She just knows the feeling is enormous, and that the only way she can imagine removing it is to be certain of getting everything right. Which is impossible, which is why the worry never switches off.

Separate the two problems

There is the work problem — what she knows. And there is the worry problem — what happens to her body when she is tested. Parents spend all their energy on the first and none on the second, then wonder why a bright girl freezes in the hall. Both need a plan. Only one of them is solved by more worksheets.

Revise in twenty-minute blocks

  • Twenty minutes on, five off, and stop at four blocks. A tired brain rehearses mistakes.
  • Little and often beats long and grim. Four short sessions across a week will beat one two-hour Sunday.
  • Practise the feeling, not just the facts. Once a week, do a mini paper under real conditions — timed, quiet, no phone, no help. She needs to meet the racing heart before the day, so it is familiar rather than frightening.
  • Finish on a win. End every session with something she can already do. Confidence is part of the syllabus.

The night before and the morning of

No new content the night before — it only manufactures panic. Lay out clothes, pens and water. Eat something ordinary. Sleep at the normal time, even if she says she needs to cram; a tired brain loses more marks than a missing fact.

In the car, do not say “just do your best” if your family’s version of best has always meant perfect. Say instead: “You have done the work. Whatever the paper says, I am already proud of how you have handled this week.” Then talk about something else. Give her brain a job that is not the exam.

Three sentences that lower the stakes

  1. “This test measures one morning, not your whole brain.” — because it does, and she needs to hear an adult say it.
  2. “If you go blank, that is your body, not your memory. Breathe out, then start with question one.” — a plan for the freeze is worth more than a revision hour.
  3. “There is nothing you could score that would change how I see you.” — this is the one they remember at 25.
“Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters.”COLOSSIANS 3:23

That verse is a gift to an anxious girl, because it moves the audience. Not the league table, not the tutor, not the group chat — God, who already knows her and is not marking her. Effort becomes worship instead of audition. For a Christian parent, that reframe is often the whole conversation.

When exam stress needs more than a talk

If she is vomiting before school, if panic is spreading into everyday life, if she is refusing to sit tests at all, or if her mood stays low after the exams finish, speak to your GP. Exam season can uncover anxiety that was already there, and it deserves proper care, not just a pep talk.

And if the knowledge is fine but the fear is not, that is exactly the space a kid’s life coach works in: rehearsing the frozen moment, building a calm-down routine she can actually use at a desk, and helping her separate who she is from what she scores.
